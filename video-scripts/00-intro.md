# Ep 00 · A website isn't for looking finished. It's for converting.

**Length:** about 8 minutes (about 1,150 spoken words)
**Goal:** change how the viewer judges a website (by results, not looks), show how the idea of a
website has changed, and introduce Brightfold as the project the whole series builds.
**Links in description:** /lab/brightfold/path/ · /lab/brightfold/

---

## 1. Hook (0:00–0:40)

**On screen:** two website hero sections side by side. Left: dark, glossy, 3D animation, the
headline "The future of work, reimagined." Right: plain white, the headline "Write client proposals
in 10 minutes, not a day," one orange button that says "Book a demo," and three client logos underneath.

**Say:**
"Which of these websites is better? Most people pick the left one. It looks expensive. But ask
one question: after five seconds, do you know what it sells, who it's for, and what to do next?
The left one, no. The right one, yes. That's the whole series in one picture. A website isn't
there to look finished. It's there to convert."

## 2. What conversion means (0:40–2:00)

**On screen:** a simple funnel: Visit → Understand → Trust → Act → Follow-up. Each word appears in turn.

**Say:**
"Conversion means a visitor does the one thing the business needs: books a call, starts a trial,
buys. For a B2B company, that's usually a demo call.

A page that converts does four things.
It's **clear**: in five seconds you know what it is, who it's for and what to do.
It's **credible**: there's proof next to every ask, like logos, numbers and quotes.
It's **easy**: one main button, a short form, and it's fast on a phone.
And it's **measured**: every click that matters is tracked, so you can see what works and fix what doesn't.

Notice that 'beautiful' isn't on that list. Design matters a lot, but it serves these four. It doesn't replace them."

## 3. The evolution of the website (2:00–4:30)

**On screen:** a horizontal timeline with five eras. Each era shows a typical screenshot (recreated, not
copied) and the one sentence of what a website was *for* at that time.

**Say:**
"To see why this matters now, look at how the idea of a website has changed.

**1990s: a document online.** Pages written in HTML by hand, later in Dreamweaver and FrontPage.
Having a website at all was the achievement.

**2000s: a digital brochure.** Flash intros, 'skip intro' buttons, template sites. The goal was to look
impressive. That habit of judging a site by how it looks comes from here, and it's still with us.

**2010s: mobile and Google.** Phones arrived. Sites had to be responsive and findable. WordPress themes
and SEO. The site now had to *work*, not just exist.

**Late 2010s: a sales tool.** No-code tools like Webflow and Framer meant marketers could change
pages every week. Add Google Analytics, A/B tests and CRM forms, and the website became something you
measure and improve, like a salesperson that works around the clock.

**Now: AI-assisted.** AI helps with research, copy and code. With Claude Code, one designer can design,
build, connect analytics and ship. And the site itself can talk back: try the product, answer questions,
draft something for you.

Here's the point. The tools got faster every decade, but the job kept moving the same way: from
*showing* to *selling* to *measuring*. AI doesn't change the job. It means one person can now do all of it."

## 4. What that means for designers (4:30–5:30)

**On screen:** four role cards: UX designer, Web designer, AI-fluent designer, Growth partner.

**Say:**
"So what does a client need from a designer today? Not just screens. They need someone who understands the
customer, designs the product experience, builds a site that converts, measures it, and keeps improving it.
That's four roles: UX, web, AI and growth. This series covers all four, on one project."

## 5. Meet Brightfold (5:30–7:15)

**On screen:** the /lab/brightfold/ page. Scroll slowly: the business, then the 13-chapter map, then the
design system chapter with the light and dark cards.

**Say:**
"The project is called Brightfold. It's made up, and I say that clearly on the site: no real clients, no
fake results. Brightfold sells proposal software to small consultancies. Today they write proposals in
Word, it takes a day, and they never know if the client read it.

I'm building all of Brightfold in public, in 13 chapters and five stages: Idea, Product, Website, Growth
and Client. Strategy and research. A design system, already built: one token file that drives Figma, the
product and the website. Product screens and an AI feature. A marketing site with motion. Analytics, a CRM
form and an A/B test. Accessibility and a usability test. And finally, how to win and keep a client.

Each chapter has an episode. Each episode is a full walkthrough, at least five minutes, showing the real files."
